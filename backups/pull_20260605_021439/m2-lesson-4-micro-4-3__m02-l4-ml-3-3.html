Spotting Suspicious Labels

Patterns in labels reveal patterns in the people who made them.

What does a bad labeling job look like before you train?

Before You Watch

Audit labels by hand, every dataset, the first hour.

“Pull 50 random rows. Read the labels. Check who labeled them and how. Spend an hour at the start and save weeks later. The teams that do this catch problems before they become models.”

Concept Companion

Four red flags in any label set.

These four patterns show up across very different label sets and almost always indicate a problem worth investigating.

Labels cluster around one category

Heavy skew

A toxicity dataset where 90 percent of toxic labels are on one topic. Usually labeler bias.

A single person did most of the work

One labeler dominant

Their personal style becomes the model's style. Quality cannot exceed one person's consistency.

Two labelers said different things and no one settled it

Unresolved disagreement

Pure noise. The model gets contradictory answers and has to guess which to trust.

Compare

Healthy vs suspicious labeling setups.

A healthy setup has multiple labelers, clear guidelines, and a quality check. A suspicious one is missing at least one of those.

3 labelers, clear guidelines, sampled quality check

Likely outcome

Clean labels, ready to train

1 labeler, vague guidelines, no quality check

Likely outcome

Labels reflect that one person; bias likely

Crowdsourced, paid per item, strict time limit

Likely outcome

Speed errors and shortcuts; sample-audit needed

Auto-generated by a previous model

Likely outcome

Previous model's errors get baked in; almost always bad
