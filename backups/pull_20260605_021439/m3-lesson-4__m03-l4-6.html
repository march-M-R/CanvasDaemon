The Core Question

Good enough is not a number. It is a relationship to stakes.

A model can be 80% accurate and shippable for one purpose, and 99% accurate and dangerous for another. The accuracy required depends on what happens when the model is wrong, who pays the cost, and whether a human will catch the mistake.

Animation of a card being stamped with a quality verdict

Good Enough Defined

Accurate enough that the cost of remaining errors is acceptable to the people who will live with them.

The Three Zones of the Data Curve Click to expand or collapse this section.

Where you are on the curve changes what to do next

Three zones. Three different strategies.

The accuracy curve does not climb forever. It climbs steeply at first, slowly in the middle, and almost not at all at the top. Where you are decides what your next hour of work should be.

1

0 to 30 samples per class

Steep Zone

Every new sample makes a visible difference. Just keep capturing. Variety matters less here.
2

30 to 100 per class

Helpful Zone

Gains are real but smaller. New angles, new lighting, new backgrounds become the new fuel.
3

100+ per class

Flat Zone

Volume barely moves the needle. Only targeted samples that fix specific failures still help.

Key Point

Where you are on the curve is more important than how many samples you have. A model with 30 varied samples beats a model with 200 near-duplicates.

Patching Weakness vs Adding Random Data Two iteration strategies with very different returns.

On the flat zone, the type of sample you add matters far more than the count. Random samples and targeted samples produce very different next models.

Compare on Random Additions Targeted Additions
Example Capture: 50 more photos Capture: 10 photos of the worst case
Outcome Result: same accuracy ± 1% Result: 4-8% accuracy gain
Note Effort wasted. Hour well spent.
Why it matters Adding 50 photos that look like the photos the model already gets right teaches it nothing new. Accuracy nudges up by a fraction of a point, if at all. The hour was spent on something that did not move. Adding 10 photos that look like the off-diagonal failure in the matrix forces the model to learn the case it was missing. The gain is concentrated where it matters and visible in the very next test run.

The Four 'I Would Not Trust This For' Statements Open this to see the discipline of honest limits.

Honest Limits

Naming what you would not do with it is what makes it real.

An honest Model Card includes at least four limits. Writing them is uncomfortable. Reading them is what makes the next user trust you.

1

Limit 1

Conditions outside training

I would not trust this model in lighting, angles, or environments it was not trained on.
2

Limit 2

Stakes above the bar

I would not trust this for medical, legal, or financial decisions that affect real people.
3

Limit 3

Subgroups underrepresented in data

I would not trust this for inputs that look like a subgroup my training data barely covered.
4

Limit 4

Without human review

I would not trust this for any decision that does not have a human in the loop, where stakes warrant it.

Result

Four sentences. They take ten minutes to write. They are the difference between 'I built a classifier' and 'I shipped a documented system.'

Common Iteration Mistakes Open this section to review the traps.

Common beliefs that fall apart on contact with real data.

Myth

Iterating forever

Reality

There is always one more improvement. Set a 'good enough' bar before you start, tied to the stakes. Ship when you hit it. Document the rest as known limits.

Myth

Hiding the weaknesses

Reality

The temptation is to write a confident Model Card that leaves out the embarrassing parts. Future-you, and future-users, will pay for that omission. Be honest now.

Myth

Treating accuracy as the only metric

Reality

A model that is 92% accurate and never works on darker-skinned hands is not a 92% model for everyone. Disaggregate accuracy by subgroup whenever your data lets you.

Key Takeaways

What you should remember from Lesson 4

Target your worst confusion. Adding samples to the class the model already gets right wastes time. Add samples that target the failure you can see in the matrix.

Diminishing returns is real. The jump from 5 to 30 samples per class is huge. The jump from 30 to 300 is much smaller. The jump from 300 to 3000 is barely measurable.

The Model Card is the deliverable. Not the model. A model without an honest Model Card is a black box. A Model Card without a model is at least a written commitment to honesty.
