The Core Question

Features and labels are the only two things a supervised model ever sees.

Strip away the architecture, the frameworks, the buzzwords. Every supervised model trains on pairs: (features, label). Features describe the example. The label is the answer. The model is just a function that learns to map one to the other. The quality of those two things sets the ceiling on everything that follows.

Animation of inputs being sorted into labeled categories

Features and Labels Defined

Features are the inputs that describe each example. The label is the answer the model is trying to predict.

Three Properties of a Useful Feature Click to expand or collapse this section.

Signal, availability, ethics

A good feature passes all three checks.

Not every column in your spreadsheet should be a feature. The strong ones share three properties. Miss any one and the column hurts more than it helps.

1

Mechanically related to the target

Signal

A meaningful causal or correlational link to what you are predicting. Random numbers do not count, even if they happen to correlate.
2

Known at prediction time

Available

If you only know this feature after the target, it is not usable in production. This is the leakage trap in disguise.
3

Safe to use in this context

Ethical

Not a protected attribute. Not a proxy for one. Not something that would harm the user if the prediction were wrong.

Key Point

Feature selection is not just a technical step. It is where ethics, leakage prevention, and signal hunting all happen at the same time.

Good Labels vs Bad Labels What separates a label set the model can learn from.

Labels are the answer key the model studies from. If the answer key is wrong, the student learns the wrong thing. The model has no way to know which labels to trust.

Compare on Good Labels Bad Labels
Example From multiple labelers Single labeler under speed pressure
Outcome With clear guidelines and disagreement resolved Vague guidelines, no quality check
Note Sampled and quality-checked. Speed paid, accuracy assumed.
Why it matters Multiple labelers agreeing on the same example raises confidence. Clear guidelines reduce ambiguity. A sampling-based quality check catches drift early. This is what real production data pipelines invest in. One labeler under time pressure with vague rules produces consistent errors. The same person's biases become the model's biases at scale. No quality check means the team never finds out until production.
Feature Engineering: From Raw to Useful Open this to see how raw inputs become real features.

Transformation

Raw data is rarely the right input.

Most of the time, the data you have is not in the form the model needs. Feature engineering is the work of transforming raw fields into signals that carry the actual pattern.

From Raw Data to Features

Three common kinds of transformation: combine columns, extract from text or dates, and aggregate across rows. Each takes 10 lines of code. Each can lift accuracy more than swapping the model architecture.

Step 1

Combine

Price minus neighborhood median. Distance between two points. The difference is often more useful than either original column.

Step 2

Extract

Pull hour and day-of-week from a timestamp. Pull length and exclamation count from a tweet.

Step 3

Aggregate

Average transactions per month per user. Total tags per photo. Rolling counts over time.

Step 4

Encode

Turn 'red, blue, green' into three 0/1 columns. The model cannot read text directly.

The Five Label-Quality Habits Open this to see how production teams keep labels honest.

Label Discipline

Five habits, applied every project.

Bad labels are the most expensive bug in machine learning. The teams that ship reliable models all share the same five habits, applied without exception.

1

Habit 1

Have written labeling guidelines

Borderline cases get specific rules. Without guidelines, every labeler invents their own.
2

Habit 2

Use at least two labelers per item

Disagreement surfaces ambiguity. Single-labeler data hides the noise.
3

Habit 3

Sample-audit by hand

Pull 50 random labels and check them yourself. Errors show up fast.
4

Habit 4

Track inter-labeler agreement

If two labelers agree 95% of the time, you have a strong signal. If 60%, the guidelines are broken.

Habit 5

Keep a list of known label errors and review it before retraining. Past mistakes are the strongest predictor of future ones in the same pipeline.

Common Feature and Label Mistakes Open this section to review the traps.

Common beliefs that fall apart on contact with real data.

Myth

'Throw everything in and let the model decide'

Reality

More features can mean more noise, more risk of leakage, and slower training. Every feature you include is a choice. Defend it.

Myth

'The labels are correct because the system already had them'

Reality

Pre-existing labels often reflect old decisions, old biases, or shortcuts. 'It was already labeled' is not a quality check.

Myth

'Feature engineering is what algorithms are for'

Reality

Modern algorithms still benefit hugely from good features. The teams that ship strong models almost always invest more in features than in architecture changes.

Key Takeaways

What you should remember from Lesson 2

Features carry the signal. Picking the right columns matters more than picking the right algorithm in most real projects.

One bad label is noise. Many bad labels are bias. Audit who labeled the data, how, and under what pressure. The labels are the model's teacher.

Feature engineering is half the work. Raw data rarely teaches a model anything useful until it is transformed into the right shape.
