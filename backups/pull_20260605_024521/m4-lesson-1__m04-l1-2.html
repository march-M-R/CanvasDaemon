The Core Question

Before we audit AI, we need to define fairness.

An accurate model can still be deeply unfair. An overall accuracy number can hide a pattern where one group is hurt much more often than another. Fairness is a design choice that must be made explicitly, not assumed.

Animation representing balance and fairness

Fairness Defined

An algorithm is fair when its design and outcomes are evaluated separately for every group it affects, not just on aggregate.

Three Definitions of Fairness Click to expand or collapse this section.

Multiple Definitions, One Choice

Fairness is not one thing.

Researchers and ethicists use at least three different definitions of algorithmic fairness. They sound similar. They often conflict. Pick one and you have implicitly traded away the others.

Equal outcomes by group

Group fairness

Similar accuracy or false-positive rates across protected groups.

Similar people, similar predictions

Individual fairness

Two applicants with similar profiles should get similar decisions.

Fair process, not just outcome

Procedural fairness

People can see, contest, and appeal the model's decision.

Key Point: Researchers have mathematically proven you cannot satisfy every fairness definition at once. Whoever designs the model is choosing which trade-offs to make.

Disparate Impact: The Same Rule, Different Outcomes How 'neutral' algorithms produce unequal results.

Two real cases. Same algorithm logic. Very different outcomes for different groups, even though the model never saw the protected attribute directly.

What the model sees

Inputs the model uses

Zip code, income, school name

No mention of race or gender.

On paper, the algorithm is neutral. It only uses features that any analyst would consider professional. The audit report shows zero references to protected attributes.

What actually happens

Applicants denied at 3x higher rate

One zip code vs neighboring ones

Same income, same credit score.

Zip code is highly correlated with race in many places. The model never 'saw' race, but it discovered the pattern through a proxy and produced racially uneven outcomes.

The Proxy Problem

You cannot remove bias by deleting one column.

Stripping sensitive attributes from a dataset does not make the model blind to them. The model finds proxies in the remaining data and discriminates through those instead.

Training Data (sensitive attribute removed)

Even after race, gender, and age are deleted, the data still contains features highly correlated with all three.

What gets removed

Race, gender, age, religion, disability status. The obvious protected attributes.

Zip code

Strongly correlates with race in many places. The model learns racial patterns through geography.

School name

Carries information about socioeconomic background, race, and class.

Browsing or purchase history

Reveals demographic group membership with surprising accuracy.

Proxy: A feature that stands in for a sensitive attribute.

Correlation: Why proxies work even when they are not the protected feature.

Feedback loop: Biased outcomes generate more biased training data.

Audit: The only way to find proxies is to test outcomes by group.

The Fairness Audit

Four questions. Real numbers per answer.

Before any model touches a real decision, a responsible team answers these four questions in writing, with specific numbers.

Q1

Are outcomes equal across groups?

Disaggregate accuracy and error rates by every protected attribute the model affects.

Q2

What proxies might exist?

List the features that correlate with sensitive attributes, even if those attributes were removed.

Q3

Who has the highest false rate?

The group with the highest false-positive or false-negative rate is the group paying the most cost.

Q4

Can people appeal the decision?

Procedural fairness matters as much as outcome fairness. People need a recourse path.

Result: A fairness audit that answers all four with specific numbers is a real audit. Anything less is a press release.

Common Fairness Misconceptions Open this section to review the traps.

'We removed the sensitive attribute, so we are fair'

Proxies remain. Removing race from a dataset does not stop a model from learning racial patterns through zip code, school name, or browsing history.

'90% accurate means it works for everyone'

Aggregate accuracy can hide a 95-70 split between groups. The 70% group is paying for the 95% group's performance number.

'It is the data's fault, not ours'

Choosing to deploy a model trained on biased data is itself a choice. The team that ships the model owns the outcomes, not the dataset.

Key Takeaways

What you should remember from Lesson 1

Aggregate accuracy hides harm. A 90% model can be 95% accurate for one group and 70% for another. Always disaggregate.

Sensitive attributes have proxies. Removing 'race' or 'gender' does not remove bias; zip code, school name, and job history often carry it.

The model's mistakes cost the user, not the developer. Whoever is underrepresented in training data is usually the one who pays.
